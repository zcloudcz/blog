The developer's job is gradually moving away from writing code itself and towards design, decision making and ownership of the final product. And the data is already showing that shift.

## What the data says

[Anthropic analysed 500,000 coding interactions](https://www.anthropic.com/research/impact-software-development). For Claude Code, 79 % of interactions were classified as automation: the AI was doing the work directly instead of just advising a human.

According to the [Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai), 84 % of developers use AI or plan to, and 51 % of professional developers use it daily.

At the same time there is considerable distrust in the accuracy of AI output:

- roughly 33 % of respondents trust AI output,
- roughly 46 % distrust it.

These numbers are the sum of "somewhat" and "highly" trust/distrust answers, so they are rounded.

It is also interesting to see where developers do not want AI yet:

- 76 % do not plan to use AI for deployment and monitoring,
- 69 % do not plan to use it for project planning.

## A new division of labour

A fairly clear split is emerging. AI takes over a growing share of implementation: endpoints, components, tests, boilerplate, routine refactoring.

Human value moves elsewhere:

- understanding the business problem,
- product thinking and analysis,
- architecture and technology trade-offs,
- directing AI agents,
- reviewing and validating the result,
- security,
- getting the application all the way to production,
- monitoring and working with real traffic.

## AI as an amplifier

[DORA 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report) describes AI as an amplifier of the existing engineering process. It helps well-functioning teams perform better. In a poorly set up process it amplifies the problems instead. Higher AI adoption is associated with higher delivery throughput and product performance, but still also with lower software delivery stability.

So producing code faster is not enough on its own. The cheaper and faster implementation gets, the more important good specifications, architecture, automated tests, review, observability and the ability to ship a change to production safely become.

## Back to software engineers

Maybe we are not moving from programmers back to analysts. Maybe we are moving from programmers back to software engineers. People who can take a business problem and carry it:

from requirement → through design → through AI agents → to a working product in production.

The ability to write C#, Java or TypeScript will not disappear. It just may no longer be the most valuable part of the job.

Code is getting cheaper. Decisions, accountability and the ability to deliver working software are not.
